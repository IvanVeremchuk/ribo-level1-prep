Four-week plan (about 90 minutes a day)

Day 1

Book the exam for about 30 days out, through the Insurance Institute of Ontario or IBAO. A fixed date keeps you on schedule. Download every document under Free sources.

Week 1

General + broker rules. Course lessons 1–2.

  • Read the RIB Act, Reg 991 (code of conduct, trust accounts, records) and By-Law 3 (registration). Skim Regs 989 and 990.
  • Make flashcards: every regulator and what it does, every principle (indemnity, subrogation, utmost good faith, insurable interest).
  • Practice tab: General, 20 questions a day.
Week 2

Auto. Lesson 3. This is the most detailed domain, so read the OAP 1 itself, section by section.

  • Draw the OAP 1 map (Sections 3–7) on one page from memory.
  • Learn the OPCF table in lesson 3 by number and purpose.
  • Read the FSRA July 2026 accident benefits summary. Know what is mandatory now and what is optional.
  • Do 10 DCPD and fault-split calculations.
Week 3

Habitational, commercial, travel. Lessons 4–6.

  • Read one full homeowners wording slowly: insuring agreement, coverages A–H, exclusions, conditions, special limits.
  • Do 10 co-insurance problems until the formula is automatic.
  • Commercial is new since 2025 and is 20% of the score. Focus on concepts, not wording details.
Week 4

Exam practice. Lessons 7–8.

  • Do RIBO's official sample PDF timed, then at least two timed mock exams on the Practice tab.
  • For every miss, find the rule in the source document and write one line about it.
  • Aim for 85%+ in practice. The real exam feels harder than practice.
  • Last two days: flashcards and the OPCF table only. Sleep.

1. How insurance works

General · 25%
  • Insurance pools many people's premiums to pay the losses of the few. It is a contract of indemnity: it restores you to where you were before the loss, no better.
  • Insurable interest: you must suffer a financial loss if the property is damaged. For property it must exist at the time of loss.
  • Utmost good faith: both sides must disclose material facts. Material misrepresentation or non-disclosure can void the policy or defeat a claim.
  • Subrogation: after paying, the insurer takes over the insured's right to sue whoever caused the loss. Contribution: two policies covering the same loss share it.
  • Peril = cause of loss (fire, theft). Hazard = condition that makes a loss more likely or worse. Physical hazard is tangible (oily rags, old wiring). Moral hazard is about the person's honesty or motives (over-insuring a failing business).
  • Distribution: brokers represent the client and place business with many insurers; agents represent the insurer (captive agents only one); direct writers sell to the public with their own employees.
  • Insurer types: stock companies (owned by shareholders), mutuals (owned by policyholders), Lloyd's (a marketplace where syndicates underwrite, not one company), MGAs (given authority by insurers to underwrite and bind, often for specialty risks).
  • Facility Association: the auto market of last resort, funded by all auto insurers in the province, for drivers who can't get coverage in the regular market.
  • Binding authority: the broker's power, granted by contract with each insurer, to put coverage in force immediately within set limits.
  • Loss ratio = claims incurred ÷ premiums earned. Rates rise with inflation, rebuild costs, climate losses (water, hail, wildfire), fraud and new technology like expensive car sensors.
  • Canada uses common law, except Quebec, which uses the Civil Code for private law.

2. Rules for brokers

General · 25%
BodyRole
RIBOSelf-regulatory body for general insurance brokers in Ontario: licensing, conduct, complaints, discipline. Set up under the Registered Insurance Brokers Act.
FSRARegulates insurers, agents and auto rates in Ontario. Approves auto rates and underwriting rules, and enforces the Take All Comers rule.
CCIRNational council of insurance regulators. Sets shared guidance such as Fair Treatment of Customers.
CISRONational group of intermediary (agent/broker) regulators. Wrote the Code of Conduct for Insurance Intermediaries.
  • Level 1 licence = "acting under supervision". You must work for a RIBO-registered brokerage under a Principal Broker. You can't run your own brokerage.
  • Know what's in the RIB Act, Reg 991 (code of conduct, misconduct, trust accounts, records) and By-Law 3 (registration, and changes you must report, such as changing brokerages).
  • Premium money is trust money. It goes into a trust account and can't pay the brokerage's own bills.
  • Disclose conflicts of interest before recommending: insurer ownership of the brokerage, commissions, contingent profit-sharing and fees.
  • Tied selling (making one product a condition of buying another) is prohibited. Negative option billing (charging for something the client didn't agree to) is prohibited.
  • Privacy: under PIPEDA, collect personal information only for a stated purpose, with consent (express or implied), and protect it. CASL requires consent before sending commercial electronic messages.
  • E&O (errors and omissions) insurance covers a broker's professional negligence. Example: forgetting to add an endorsement the client asked for.
  • Fraud: you must not take part in or ignore it. Report it under brokerage procedures and the insurer's rules.
  • Licences renew every year (Oct 1–Sep 30). RIBO requires continuing education hours, and tracking them is your job.

3. Personal auto (OAP 1)

Auto · 25%
OAP 1What it does
Section 3Third-party liability. You hurt someone or damage their property. Minimum $200,000, most people buy $1–2M.
Section 4Accident benefits (no-fault). Pays for your own injuries, whoever caused the crash. Details are in the SABS regulation.
Section 5Uninsured automobile. Covers injury caused by an uninsured or unidentified driver, and damage to your car by an identified uninsured driver.
Section 6DCPD (direct compensation, property damage). Your own insurer pays for your car to the extent you are not at fault, for collisions in Ontario with another insured vehicle. You can opt out with OPCF 49.
Section 7Loss or damage to your own car. Options: All Perils, or Collision/Upset plus Comprehensive, or Specified Perils (named perils only, e.g. fire, theft, hail).
July 1, 2026 change: only medical, rehabilitation and attendant care accident benefits stay mandatory ($65,000 combined for non-catastrophic injuries, $1,000,000 for catastrophic). Income replacement, non-earner, caregiver, death and funeral and the other benefits became optional. Optional benefits apply only to the named insured, spouse, dependants and listed drivers. Auto insurance is now first payer for medical and rehab, ahead of workplace health plans. Exam items may still be updating, so read FSRA's summary and check which version your exam provider tests.

20Loss of use / transportation replacement (rental car after a covered loss)
23ALienholder (mortgage) clause, protecting the lender's interest

43 / 43ARemoving depreciation deduction (new car replaced or paid at new value)
44RFamily Protection. Covers you when the at-fault driver's liability limit is too low, up to your own limit
47ROptional accident benefits endorsement (new with the 2026 reform)
49Agreement Not to Recover, the DCPD opt-out
  • Fault Determination Rules (O. Reg. 668) set fault from diagrams, not from what drivers say. Rear-ending a car in the same lane = 100% at fault. Chain reactions and lane changes have their own rules.
  • DCPD math: payment = damage × (100% − your fault %), minus any DCPD deductible. The at-fault share is paid only under Collision or All Perils, after its deductible.
  • Statutory conditions: report material changes, cooperate on claims, file proof of loss on time. The insurer can cancel with 5 days' notice delivered personally or 15 days by registered mail. The insured can cancel any time.
  • Territory: Canada, the United States and vessels travelling between ports of the two.
  • Compulsory Automobile Insurance Act: every car on an Ontario road must be insured. Motor Vehicle Accident Claims Fund: pays victims when no insurance applies, as the last resort.
  • Take All Comers: an insurer must offer coverage to anyone who meets its FSRA-approved underwriting rules.
  • Sharing economy: a personal policy doesn't cover carrying paying passengers or commercial delivery unless the insurer adds coverage for it. Changes in use are material and must be reported.

4. Home, condo and tenant policies

Habitational · 25%
CoverageWhat it pays
ADwelling building
BDetached private structures (shed, detached garage, fence)
CPersonal property (contents)
DAdditional living expense / fair rental value when the home can't be lived in
ELegal liability (you're legally responsible for injury or damage to others)
FVoluntary medical payments to others (paid regardless of fault)
GVoluntary payment for damage to others' property
HVoluntary compensation for residence employees
  • Forms: Basic = named perils for building and contents. Broad = all risks on the building, named perils on contents. Comprehensive = all risks on both. "All risks" still has exclusions.
  • Policy types: homeowners, tenants (contents + liability, no building), condo unit owners (contents, improvements, loss assessment, deductible assessment), seasonal/secondary, vacant.
  • Water: sudden escape of water from plumbing is usually covered. Sewer backup, overland water (flooding from lakes, rivers, surface runoff) and ground water need endorsements. Sell them together, because one event often involves several causes (concurrent causation).
  • Special limits cap jewellery, furs, cash, collectibles, bicycles and similar items, often only for theft. To insure full value, schedule the item.
  • Vacancy: if the home is vacant more than 30 consecutive days, coverage for some perils (such as vandalism, glass breakage and water damage) is suspended. Freezing losses while the home is unheated are covered only if someone checks it as the wording requires or the water is shut off and drained.
  • Valuation: actual cash value (ACV) = replacement cost minus depreciation. Replacement cost usually pays only after you actually repair or replace.
  • Material changes to report: renting out part of the home, short-term rentals (Airbnb), a home-based business, renovations, wood stoves, a vacancy.
  • Recreational: homeowners policies limit boats by horsepower or length and limit trailers. Bigger boats need a watercraft policy.
  • Cyber / identity theft endorsements pay expenses to restore your identity and credit.

Co-insurance formula

Payment = (Insurance carried ÷ Insurance required) × Loss
Insurance required = Value × Co-insurance %
Never more than the policy limit. If carried ≥ required, pay the full loss.

Example: home worth $500,000, 80% clause, so $400,000 required. Insured for $300,000. Loss of $40,000. Payment = 300 ÷ 400 × 40,000 = $30,000.

5. Commercial insurance

Commercial · 20%
  • Who is the insured: a sole proprietor is personally liable for business debts; a partnership's partners are generally liable too; a corporation is a separate legal entity.
  • Property: building, equipment, stock. Insure to rebuild value, not market value. Co-insurance clauses (often 80–90%) are common.
  • Business interruption: replaces lost profit and continuing expenses while the business is shut after an insured property loss. Extra expense pays the extra cost of staying open (temporary premises).
  • Equipment breakdown (boiler and machinery): sudden mechanical or electrical breakdown, which property forms exclude.
  • Crime: employee dishonesty, money and securities, safe burglary, fraud.
  • CGL (commercial general liability): A = bodily injury and property damage, B = personal and advertising injury, C = medical payments, D = tenants' legal liability (damage to premises you rent).
  • Occurrence form: responds if the injury or damage happens during the policy period, whenever the claim is made. Claims-made form: responds if the claim is made during the policy period (after any retroactive date). Professional liability is usually claims-made.
  • Professional liability / E&O: errors in professional services. D&O: directors' and officers' wrongful acts in running the company. Cyber: data breach, ransomware, privacy liability.
  • Builders' risk: buildings under construction. Surety bonds have three parties: principal, obligee and surety. They guarantee performance and are not insurance for the principal.
  • Subscription policy: several insurers each take a percentage of one risk and each is liable only for its share.
  • Commercial auto: OAP 1 for individually rated commercial vehicles, fleets, OAP 4 garage policy for dealers and repair shops (customers' cars in their care). SPF 6 covers non-owned auto liability, for employees driving their own cars on business. CVOR is needed for trucks over 4,500 kg and buses.
  • Certificates of insurance only show that coverage exists. They don't change or add to the policy.

6. Travel health

Travel · 5%
  • OHIP pays only a small part of emergency costs outside Canada. Travel medical insurance covers the rest.
  • Types: emergency medical, trip cancellation/interruption, baggage, single-trip vs multi-trip annual, visitors to Canada.
  • Pre-existing conditions: most policies require a condition to be stable for a set period before departure. A medication change, new symptom or test can make it "unstable".
  • Medical questionnaires must be answered accurately. A wrong answer can void coverage when you claim.
  • Claims: call the assistance company before or as soon as treatment starts. Many policies limit payment if you don't.
  • Travel advisories: going somewhere with a government "avoid non-essential travel" advisory already in place can exclude related claims.

7. Working with clients and claims

All domains
  • Needs analysis: ask open questions first, then closed ones to confirm. Look for exposures the client didn't mention.
  • Recommend and document: explain options, limits and exclusions. Disclose conflicts first. Write every recommendation and every rejected option in the file. This is your best E&O defence.
  • Binding: bind only within your authority, then confirm in writing to the client and insurer.
  • Renewals and changes: compare old and new coverage, flag material changes, deliver documents on time.
  • Claims: collect the facts and report first notice of loss to the insurer quickly. Explain the process. Don't promise coverage, because the adjuster decides. Advocate for the client if there's a dispute.
  • Conflict: stay calm, listen, escalate to your supervisor or the insurer when needed. Clients can complain to RIBO or FSRA.

8. Exam technique

Exam day
  • You get 3 hours for 115 questions, about 1.5 minutes each. Flag hard questions and move on.
  • Read the last line of the question first ("Which is NOT…", "What would the insurer pay?").
  • Words like ONLY, MUST and NOT decide the answer. RIBO puts them in capitals.
  • For math questions, write the formula in the on-screen notes before entering numbers.
  • For "what should the broker do" questions, the right answer is usually the one that discloses, documents, reports or tells the insurer.
  • Fifteen pilot questions don't count. A strange question may be one of them, so don't panic.
  • Virtual exam: clear the desk completely, run the system check the day before. Only one 5-minute break, and the clock keeps running.
